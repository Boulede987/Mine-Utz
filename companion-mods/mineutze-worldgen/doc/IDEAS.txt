## 1. A guaranteed Pareto improvement is possible — but only if you make "don't get worse" a hard constraint

The important distinction is:

> **Don't optimize an aggregate objective and hope it behaves fairly. Put the baseline's per-face performance into the feasible set.**

This is a standard idea behind ε-constraint / lexicographic multiobjective optimization and equitable resource allocation: optimize one criterion while imposing explicit bounds on the competing criteria. Lexicographic minimax is also an established formulation for equitable allocation. ([PubsOnline][1])

### Your baseline gives you an unusually nice reference point

For every face \(i\), define

$$
b_i = |r_i^{baseline}|
       = \left|\frac{A_i^{baseline}-T_i}{T_i}\right|.
$$

Now solve:

$$
\boxed{
\min_w F(w)
}
$$

subject to

$$
\boxed{
|r_i(w)| \le b_i
\qquad\forall i
}
$$

plus your existing geometric constraints.

* sum of squared normalized errors;

**The baseline itself is feasible.**

No 3.44× surprise is possible if baseline was 1.98× for that face.

## Bottom line

What you're seeing — "any aggregate improvement comes with a worse tail" — is very likely a real structural property of the objective you're optimizing. But your problem isn't that Pareto improvement is impossible. It's that every method so far has been optimizing the **wrong quantity**. The fix is to stop minimizing aggregate area error and instead enforce per-face constraints, and to treat desert and the deep-ocean family as two structurally different failures rather than one.

## Question 1: Is Pareto improvement possible, or provably impossible?

**Partially impossible — but you are not in the regime where it's impossible.**

The trade-off you keep observing has a precise mathematical source. Your Jacobian is a **graph Laplacian** (diagonal = sum of shared-edge contributions, off-diagonal = negative shared-edge terms). Every "correction" you've tried — IRLS reweighting, masking, L_p continuation — is essentially a change to the **diagonal scaling** of that Laplacian. When a face's diagonal is reduced (frozen or throttled), it acts as a *softer* constraint in the system; the neighbors' equations are unchanged, so in the next CG round the neighbors can push that face more freely.

This is not an implementation bug. It's an incompatibility between a **local modification operator** and a **globally coupled system**. The pseudoinverse of a graph Laplacian, applied to a residual, produces displacements that are weighted sums of neighbors' residuals. Weakening one face's diagonal is equivalent to introducing an asymmetric preconditioner into the pseudoinverse — it redistributes that face's error energy to its neighbors, and the direction of redistribution follows the neighbors' residual gradient, not a fairness criterion.

**But Pareto improvement is not theoretically impossible here.** What you're missing isn't an algorithm — it's a **constraint**. Every method so far solves an unconstrained problem (modulo displacement clamps) and then checks afterward who violated tolerance. Once you make "every face's |r_i| must stay below some bound" a **hard inequality constraint** in the system, the feasible region itself is clipped. Projected gradient or active-set methods on that clipped region can, in principle, reduce total violations without worsening any already-compliant face — because worsening a compliant face is blocked by the constraint.

**Key distinction:** your masking modified the *equations* (changed the diagonal). It did not add an *inequality* (bounding the final r_i). Changing the diagonal creates no feasible-region boundary; adding a constraint does.

## Question 2: Is a hard worst-case constraint feasible, and how?

**Feasible, but it requires a different solver framework.** Your system is sparse symmetric positive definite (a graph Laplacian) — that's good news. Projecting onto box constraints |r_i| ≤ τ keeps the problem convex (provided the objective stays convex). Recommended routes:

**Option A (most direct): constrained least squares with an active set.**

Formulate as
min Σ r_i²  subject to  |r_i| ≤ τ  for all i
where r_i = target_i − realized_i(w), and realized_i comes from your power diagram. Each iteration, for faces that have hit the bound, fix their residual at ±τ (equivalently, fix their effective target at target_i ∓ τ), then solve unconstrained CG on the remaining free faces. This draws a hard boundary between "compliant" and "struggling" faces: a struggling face cannot improve itself by sacrificing a compliant one.

**Note:** your earlier masking failed because it *weakened* the struggling faces. This does the opposite — it *pins the violation magnitude* of the struggling face and lets compliant faces absorb the remaining adjustment. The direction is reversed.

**Option B (if you want "fail loud" rather than "must solve"): a feasibility problem.**

Rewrite the objective as
min ε  subject to  |r_i| ≤ τ + ε  for all i
If ε* comes out significantly positive, no solution exists under the current topology that brings every face into tolerance. That's your fail-loud signal. This LP / second-order-cone form can be solved efficiently on your graph Laplacian because the constraints are per-face box constraints.

**Your masking numbers support this direction:** 201/930 was the best aggregate result, but the tail blew up. If you take that 201 as a starting point and add "no face's |r_i| may exceed 1.2× the baseline worst case" as a hard constraint, the active set will block those 3.44× blowups — at the cost of the aggregate count possibly returning to ~250, but with the tail clamped. That is the Pareto improvement you actually want (relative to the baseline's tail, not relative to masking's aggregate count).

## Question 3: The desert vs. deep-ocean topological distinction

**This is the most production-valuable finding in your entire investigation, and it directly answers "where does the tail come from."**

The fact you discovered in the masking analysis — desert has **zero range overlap** with every legal neighbor, while the four deep-ocean variants have **at least one overlapping legal neighbor** (each other, or the marginal shallow-ocean sliver) — means these two groups fail for fundamentally different reasons:

- **Desert's failure is an "infeasible" failure.** If desert's target is not within the same order of magnitude as any neighbor's target, the power diagram boundary simply does not permit desert to shrink to its target area without invading a neighbor's target area. No amount of weight tuning fixes this. This is the classic **mass-mismatch** signature in discrete optimal transport. Your CSP allowed desert to be adjacent to those neighbors, but their size declarations are geometrically incompatible.
- **Deep-ocean's failure is a "weight-competition" failure.** They are legal relative to each other and their ranges overlap, so in principle a weight configuration exists that brings them all near target. They fail because of same-tier synchronous updates — all deep-ocean faces push their weights up together and cancel each other. Your Gauss-Seidel test (282 vs. 293) only improved 11 violations, which suggests synchronization is a secondary factor. The primary factor is likely that deep-ocean faces have an unusually large **shared-edge-to-area ratio** in the power diagram (small target area → large perimeter/area → higher sensitivity to neighbor weights).

**On the topology-adjacency question:** your suspicion is correct. If the power diagram's **topology** were allowed to float (i.e., desert could "eat" some neighbors' edges and share no boundary with them at all), desert's problem might disappear. You can't float the topology, because the CSP guarantees legal adjacency. **Compromise:** in the weight solve, allow desert's shared edge with a given neighbor to **degenerate toward zero length** (rather than floating topology freely). This is equivalent, under a fixed adjacency graph, to desert "effectively not sharing a boundary" with that neighbor. This is achievable in a power diagram: when two faces' weight difference is large enough, the radical edge between them degenerates. In your Jacobian, the term ∂Area_i/∂w_j contains sharedEdgeLength_ij; when that length goes to zero, the coupling vanishes automatically. **The current solver probably never reaches this regime, because your damping is too small to move weights far enough.** Try a **large initial weight offset** (not incremental damping — directly give desert a large negative weight offset) and see whether it "releases" its edges from incompatible neighbors.

## Final recommendations

1. **Stop tuning the objective.** IRLS, L_p, and diagonal masking have all been tried; they're structurally wrong.
2. **Switch to constrained least squares:** hard constraint |r_i| ≤ τ, active set handling compliant faces, so struggling faces cannot sacrifice them.
3. **Handle desert separately:** when a "zero-range-overlap neighbor pair" is detected, allow the shared edge to degenerate (large-weight-difference regime) rather than damping gradually.
4. **Handle deep-ocean separately:** what they need is a **coordinated weight** (do multiple deep-ocean faces share a single joint weight variable?) rather than independent updates. If they are supposed to form a contiguous region in the power diagram, independent weights are the wrong parameterization.

Real Pareto improvement probably only exists **after** you stop treating desert and deep-ocean as the same kind of failure to fix.
