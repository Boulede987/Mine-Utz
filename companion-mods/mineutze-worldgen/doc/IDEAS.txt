# Diagnosis: Two Structurally Distinct Causes of Under-Realization

Based on your description, the under-realization almost certainly comes from **two independent mechanisms that happen to look identical in aggregate statistics**. They need to be separated before either can be fixed.

## Cause A — Geometric clipping by the Voronoi/Delaunay stage (area loss, not width loss)

If your geometry stage builds hex faces by clipping a *nominal* hexagon against neighbor constraints (Voronoi cells around irregular seed points, or Lloyd-relaxed Delaunay duals), then a hex's final area is:

```
A_final = A_target(w) − Σ (overlaps stolen by neighbors)
```

The stolen area is **not** proportional to the victim's target. It's proportional to the *neighbors'* push. So a biome whose declared widths are small relative to its neighbors (e.g. badlands [500,800] next to desert [900,1500]) loses a roughly constant absolute area to each shared edge — and once `A_target` itself is small, that constant loss becomes a **large fractional loss**.

Concretely: if each shared edge steals ~15% of the smaller neighbor's target area, a biome with `w̄ ≈ 650` can easily land at 25–35% of its own target when it borders wide biomes on 4–5 sides.

**Signature:** under-realization correlates with *neighbor width ratio*, not with the biome's own declared range. Isolated instances of the same biome (all neighbors narrow) hit target fine.

**Fix direction:** make the clip symmetric — solve for face areas simultaneously (a small LP/QP over edge offsets with `Σ A_i = Σ A_target_i` as a constraint), or allocate the "stolen" area back via a post-pass that grows narrow faces into their wide neighbors' slack.

## Cause B — Seed density / Poisson-disc mismatch (count loss, not per-hex loss)

If the geometry stage places one seed per hex and the number of seeds of a given biome is derived from `Σ targetArea / worldArea`, then any biome whose declared range is **narrow** gets few seeds, and Poisson-disc rejection sampling (or blue-noise) systematically **removes** seeds that fall within `r_min` of an existing seed of *any* biome. Narrow-range biomes have small `r_min` per seed, so they should survive — *unless* `r_min` is set globally (e.g. from the largest biome's min width). In that case every narrow-biome seed near a wide-biome seed is culled, and the narrow biome loses **count**, not just area.

**Signature:** under-realization correlates with the *biome's own declared range width* and with global `r_min`. The biome renders fine in regions where it's the only biome present.

**Fix direction:** make `r_min` per-biome (`r_min_i = f(w_min_i)`), or use weighted Poisson-disc / variable-radius blue noise so culling probability depends on the culled seed's own biome.

## Why they look the same in aggregate

Both produce:
- the same biome at 20–30% of target,
- the same biomes (narrow-range ones) over-represented in the failure set,
- the same "no fix found" because a fix targeting one cause doesn't move the other.

If you only tune the CSP or only tune the width sampler, you're adjusting `A_target` — which changes *both* failure modes in the same direction, so neither gets isolated.

## How to disambiguate in one experiment

Instrument the geometry stage to log, per realized hex:

| field | meaning |
|---|---|
| `A_target` | from `w` |
| `A_before_clip` | nominal hex area before neighbor clipping |
| `A_after_clip` | final rendered area |
| `n_neighbors` and their `w̄` | for Cause A |
| `seed_id`, `was_culled_by_rmin`, `r_min_used` | for Cause B |

Then:
- **Cause A** shows as `A_after_clip / A_before_clip` low, with `A_before_clip ≈ A_target`.
- **Cause B** shows as `A_before_clip / A_target` low (the seed was placed with wrong nominal area) or as missing seeds entirely.

The two ratios multiply to the observed 20–30%. If you plot them separately, the "unfixable" 20–30% splits cleanly into two populations.

## Most likely primary culprit for "as low as 20–30%"

If your geometry is Voronoi-based (most hex-grid MC mods are), **Cause A dominates**, because the fractional area loss scales as `1/A_target` — precisely the regime where narrow-range biomes live. Cause B adds a secondary hit that's harder to see because it removes instances rather than shrinking them.

The single highest-leverage change is to stop clipping symmetrically and instead **solve face areas jointly** so that `Σ A_final = Σ A_target` per local neighborhood. That alone should recover most of the missing area for narrow biomes without touching the CSP or the width sampler.

## 1. The solver I would try first: lexicographic minimax

Don't solve

$$
\min_w \sum_i r_i(w)^2
$$

where

$$
r_i=\frac{A_i-T_i}{T_i}.
$$

That's exactly where your CG experiment gets its "sacrifice one face to improve the group" behavior.

Instead solve approximately:

$$
\min_w \max_i |r_i(w)|.
$$

Even better, use **lexicographic minimax**:

1. minimize the worst normalized error;
2. freeze that optimum;
3. among solutions having that minimum worst error, minimize the second-worst error;
4. then the third-worst, etc.

That objective is almost tailor-made for your requirement.

A face at 0.25× target cannot be sacrificed merely because five neighbors can be moved from 0.99× to 1.01×. The 0.25 face dominates the objective until it has been brought substantially closer.

### But don't implement the nonlinear minimax problem directly

You already have the useful Jacobian:

$$
J_{ij}=\frac{\partial A_i}{\partial w_j}.
$$

At each iteration, linearize:

$$
r_i(w+\Delta w)
\approx r_i + \frac{1}{T_i}\sum_jJ_{ij}\Delta w_j.
$$

Then solve the small **linear program**

$$
\min_{\Delta w,t} t
$$

subject to

$$
-t \le
r_i+\frac{1}{T_i}(J\Delta w)_i
\le t
$$

for every face.

And crucially, add your geometric constraints **to this optimization**, rather than letting them clip the answer afterward.

For example:

$$
\Delta x_v \le 0.8R
$$

for every vertex-displacement constraint, plus whatever inequalities represent minimum edge lengths and simplicity/topology.

Then do a line search on the *actual recomputed polygons*.

That gives you:

**linearize → minimax solve → trial weights → rebuild geometry → accept/reject → repeat.**

This is much more appropriate than CG for your stated objective.

# 2. There's an even better version for your specific "deep-ocean cluster"

Your instrumentation gives you a very useful clue:

Suppose:


The important degree of freedom isn't necessarily:

```text
ΔwC
ΔwD
ΔwE
```

independently.

It is the **collective mode**

$$
\Delta w_C=\Delta w_D=\Delta w_E.
$$

Internal boundaries barely move under that mode, but the **outer boundary of the entire cluster moves outward** against its smaller-weight neighbors.

That suggests a very useful diagnostic/solver decomposition.

### Build a bad-cell component

For example:

```text
bad = A_i < 0.8 T_i
```

then connected components under mesh adjacency.

For each component \(C\), calculate its collective weight direction:

$$
v_C(i)=
\begin{cases}
1&i\in C\\
0&i\notin C.
\end{cases}
$$

Evaluate:

$$
Jv_C.
$$

If the component is genuinely suffering from collective contention, you should see something like:

* \(Jv_C\) ≈ 0 on internal cells,
* \(Jv_C>0\) on the component's boundary cells,
* \(Jv_C<0\) on neighboring external cells.

That's an extremely clean confirmation of what is happening.

Then give these **component modes explicit priority**.

Instead of asking the solver to discover this indirectly, say:

> "This connected group is collectively 60% under target; first find the collective weight shift that gives the group territory, subject to individual fairness."

That is basically turning the graph-Laplacian structure you already discovered into a useful solver basis.

# 3. A particularly attractive objective: worst *relative* error, not absolute area error

$$

You can go one step further and make the lower side asymmetric if undersizing is the problem:

\min \max_i

$$
\alpha>\beta.

For example, initially try:

$$
\alpha=2,\qquad\beta=1.
$$

That says:

> "I'd rather tolerate a 5% oversize than a 5% undersize."

It doesn't require changing CSP identity legality or self-adjacency.

# 4. IRLS is worth trying — but use it as a *fairness* method, not ordinary weighted least squares

Your suggested IRLS idea is good.

Ordinary least squares effectively minimizes:

$$
\sum_i r_i^2.
$$

Instead use weights that increase rapidly for persistent outliers:

$$
\min_\Delta
\sum_i q_i r_i^2
$$

where, for example,

$$
q_i =
\frac{1}{(|r_i|+\epsilon)^{p}}
$$

with \(p=1\) or \(2\).

Or a simpler engineering version:

```text
|r| < 0.10       weight = 1
0.10–0.25        weight = 2
0.25–0.40        weight = 5
0.40–0.60        weight = 15
> 0.60           weight = 50
```

Then recompute the solve.

But I would rank this **below minimax**.

IRLS still has the fundamental property that the optimizer can say:

> "I'll make this one awful cell worse because that improves several others enough."

The weights merely make that less attractive.

Minimax makes it the primary objective.

This means:

The solver doesn't blindly push until the clamp explodes.

It discovers something like:



# 6. I would make the displacement clamp an actual constraint

This is probably the biggest architectural change I'd recommend.

Right now you effectively have:

```text
solve weights
      ↓
construct geometry
      ↓
clamp geometry
```

That creates a mismatch:

> The optimizer thinks it can buy area by changing weights, but the geometry stage subsequently refuses to realize some of those changes.

From the optimizer's perspective, its model is wrong.

Instead, expose the constraint to the solver.

At a linearized step:

$$
x_v(w+\Delta w)
\approx
x_v + K_v\Delta w
$$

where \(K_v\) is the vertex-position sensitivity to weights.

Then enforce

$$
\|x_v+K_v\Delta w-x_v^0\|
\le 0.8R.
$$

You don't necessarily need a full nonlinear constraint. A conservative linearized polygonal approximation is probably sufficient.

Then the optimizer knows:

> "This face cannot get more area through this vertex because that vertex is already at its displacement limit."

That converts Mechanism A from a mysterious post-solver failure into an **active constraint**.

This is very close in spirit to capacity-constrained variants of semi-discrete OT, where hard constraints can be incorporated into the optimization rather than treated as after-the-fact corrections. ([SIAM][2])

# 7. There's a useful mathematical diagnostic hiding in your Jacobian

You already have the key equation:

$$
J_{ij}=-\frac{L_{ij}}{2D}
$$

for neighbors and

$$
J_{ii}=\sum_j\frac{L_{ij}}{2D}.
$$

That's a weighted graph Laplacian.

Therefore:

$$
J\mathbf{1}=0.
$$

This isn't just a numerical nuisance.

It gives you a powerful diagnostic.

For a connected graph, the uniform weight shift

$$
w_i\rightarrow w_i+c
$$

doesn't change the diagram.

Now consider a group \(C\). Its indicator vector \(1_C\) is approximately a **low-frequency mode** when C is a large contiguous cluster.

That's exactly your deep-ocean situation.

### Measure the cluster boundary

Define:

$$
\lambda(C)
=
\sum_{\substack{i\in C\\j\notin C}}
\frac{L_{ij}}{2D}.
$$

This is essentially the weighted cut capacity of the cluster.

Then the collective weight shift

$$
w_i\leftarrow w_i+\delta,\quad i\in C
$$

produces total first-order area transfer proportional to that cut.

So you can quantify:

> **How much "territorial leverage" does this bad cluster have against its surroundings?**

A cluster with:

* enormous total area deficit,
* very small boundary cut,

is inherently difficult to rescue individually.

That gives you a much better explanation of your deep-ocean failures than simply saying "the neighbors are also large."

# 8. This also gives you a feasibility test before solving

For a bad connected cluster \(C\), calculate:

$$
D_C=\sum_{i\in C}(T_i-A_i).
$$

Then estimate the maximum area that can be transferred through its boundary before the allowed displacement constraints activate.

Call that \(F_C\).

If

$$
D_C > F_C,
$$

then **the individual targets are geometrically infeasible under your current 0.8R constraint**, regardless of how clever the weight solver is.

That's an enormously valuable distinction.

You'd then have three classes:

### Class 1 — feasible + solver failure

```text
D_C <= F_C
```

but solver doesn't achieve targets.

→ Algorithm problem.

### Class 2 — infeasible because displacement constraints bind

```text
D_C > F_C
```

→ Mechanism A / geometry constraint.

### Class 3 — infeasible because the desired cells don't have enough external territory

e.g. a cluster nearly fills its local neighborhood with cells that themselves require area.

→ Genuine local capacity conflict.

That would let you stop tuning algorithms for cases that mathematically cannot be solved.

Once you start your correction loop,

# 10. I would also test whether your fixed adjacency is silently imposing an extra constraint

This jumped out at me from your description.

You say:

> "For 3 mutually-adjacent hex faces meeting at one mesh vertex..."

and vertices are recomputed from those fixed triples.

But a true power diagram isn't obligated to preserve your original hex-grid adjacency graph as weights change.

Increasing weights can cause:

* an edge to disappear,
* a new neighbor relation to appear,
* a cell to become nonempty/empty,
* a triple to cease being the active triple at a vertex.

The power diagram itself is defined globally by all weighted sites, not by a permanently frozen adjacency list. ([Wikipédia][4])

If your implementation keeps the original six neighbors and original triples forever, then you're not actually giving the OT solver the full feasible set of Laguerre diagrams.

You're solving:

> **"Find weights for this particular combinatorial polygon mesh whose vertices are radical centers of these predetermined triples."**

That's a substantially more constrained problem.

And it could absolutely explain why the textbook OT existence theorem doesn't seem to save some cases.

This is the first implementation detail I would audit before concluding that the power-diagram formulation itself is at fault.

# 13. One more alternative worth knowing: \(L_p\) continuation

If a full LP/minimax solver is inconvenient, there's a surprisingly good intermediate approach.

Solve a sequence:

$$
\min_w\sum_i |r_i|^p
$$

with

```text
p = 2
p = 4
p = 8
p = 16
p → ∞
```

using each solution to initialize the next.

As \(p\) grows, the objective increasingly approximates minimax.

This gives you most of the behavior you want from minimax without introducing an LP.

And because your Jacobian is already available, the implementation is relatively straightforward.

I would still prefer the explicit minimax formulation because it gives you a directly interpretable guarantee.

## Why the joint solve made outliers worse — a specific hypothesis

You said the CG solve "correctly, provably minimizes aggregate error." I'd push on that a bit. A least-squares objective weighted by squared residual actually *should* push harder on your worst offenders — a face at 0.3 ratio contributes far more to the sum-of-squares than one at 0.95, so a true unconstrained minimizer would spend disproportionate correction budget there, not less.

The more likely explanation: your Jacobian (∂Area_i/∂weight_j from the radical-center formula) models the *unclamped* system. But clamp #4 (the 0.8× displacement cap) is a hard, nonsmooth constraint that the solver has no idea exists. So on a face that's already at maxDisplacementFraction = 1.0, the solver's linear model says "increasing weight_i by X will grow area by Y" — and it allocates correction budget accordingly, discounting the *other* faces in the same linear system to balance the equations. Then the clamp gets applied post-hoc, vetoes the move on the saturated face entirely, and the "savings" the solver assumed it was getting there never materialize. You end up with faces that used to be fine now slightly worse, and the clamp-saturated faces unchanged — which matches "293→310-325" almost exactly: a few formerly-healthy faces tip over the tolerance line to pay for corrections that got clawed back by a clamp the optimizer didn't know about.

If that's right, the fix isn't a different loss function first — it's making the clamps visible to the solver. That's a real, known category: **constrained least-squares / active-set projected Newton**, where clamp #4 becomes an explicit inequality constraint in the QP rather than a post-hoc projection. Concretely: each round, before solving, mark faces already at or near their displacement cap as *fixed* (zero degrees of freedom, not part of the linear system at all — same for structurally-infeasible faces like desert, which you can detect in advance from the static range check you already have). Solve the reduced system only over the remaining free faces. This is cheap to try incrementally on top of the CG code you already have (branch preserved, per your note) — it's a masking change to which variables enter the solve, not a new algorithm.

## For the pure joint-contention case (same-tier neighbors pushing together)

Once Mechanism A and C faces are excluded from the solve as above, what's left is genuinely the individual-vs-aggregate tension you named. Two concrete options, in order of how much they reuse your existing code:

**1. IRLS on the existing CG solve (smallest change).** Reweight each face's residual by something like `1/max(current_ratio, ε)` before each CG pass, so faces further from target get proportionally more say in the objective — this pushes the aggregate L2 solve toward an L∞ (minimax) solve as iterations progress. This is literally what you flagged as untried; I'd just note it slots directly into your existing matrix-free CG without restructuring anything, so it's a cheap experiment to run before the more invasive options.

**2. Max-min water-filling (more principled, more work).** This is the standard technique from network rate-allocation / fair-share auctions for exactly "some agents are structurally capped, don't let that block fairness among the rest": each round, solve the joint system, find whichever face(s) hit a hard constraint (clamp, or zero relative headroom against all neighbors, per the weight-difference math), *freeze* them at their best achievable value, remove them from the free-variable set, and re-solve the remainder. It's your convergence-stall detector (#4 in your list) done with a real infeasibility test instead of a symptom threshold — the reason #4 misfired ("froze faces still genuinely converging") is it used round-over-round delta as a proxy for "stuck," which is noisy; testing directly against the clamp state or the Jacobian's actual null-space is not.

**3. Gauss-Seidel-style sequential ordering (cheapest to test as a diagnostic, not necessarily a shipped fix).** Since the boundary only moves on weight *differences*, and your current correction is Jacobi-style (everyone updates off last round's state simultaneously), same-tier neighbors updating in lockstep is almost definitionally why they can't gain ground on each other. Processing faces sequentially within a round — worst-ratio-first — and using each face's already-updated weight when computing its neighbor's correction in the same round would inject exactly the asymmetry that's currently absent. This won't fix desert (Mechanism C, no legal neighbor is even in range), but for something like the deep-ocean cluster it directly targets the "nobody moves relative to anyone else" failure mode, and it's a small enough change that it's worth running just to confirm the diagnosis before building the heavier machinery.

I'd try #1 or #3 first since they're near-zero-cost against your existing pipeline and would tell you quickly whether the diagnosis holds before you invest in a real active-set solver.
